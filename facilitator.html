Facilitator's Guide

From Signal
to System

How to prepare, run, grade, and review the intelligence OPD and Exercise NORTH WATCH.

This guide is for the officer or NCO running the OPD. Students do not need it. It assumes you have read the student site end to end. Doctrinal references are to FM 2-0, Intelligence (October 2026), unless stated otherwise.

01

Intent and audience

Purpose. Build analysts who use data and AI tools to work faster without lowering the standard. The standard is the nine characteristics of effective intelligence in FM 2-0, paragraphs 1-3 and 1-4. The course thesis comes straight from FM 2-0's discussion of PED versus analysis and exploitation: AI/ML can structure and fuse data, but critical thinking and contextual understanding "fundamentally require human judgment."

End state. Every cell leaves with a hosted tool it built, a fused I&W product graded against the rubric, and the habit of saying where AI was used and how it was checked.

Audience and cell size

  • Students: intelligence officers, warrant officers, and NCOs (35-series and civilians in equivalent roles). Staff officers from other warfighting functions benefit from Modules 01 and 02 and can join a cell.
  • Cells: three to five students. Mix experience levels and put at least one person who is comfortable with code in each cell that chooses Track 2 or Track 3.
  • Facilitators: one lead facilitator, plus one assistant for every three cells during practical exercises. The assistant's main job in Module 03 is the build clinic.

What students should already know

Basic intelligence requirements (PIR and indicators) and the intelligence process. No coding is required; Track 1 needs none. Students who have never used GitHub should create an account and complete GitHub's "Hello World" guide as pre-work.

02

Coordination and preparation

Do this first

Brief the plan to your intelligence oversight officer and servicing judge advocate before you schedule anything. FM 2-0, appendix C, requires a documented mission and authority for intelligence activities and intelligence oversight training before collection. This OPD is training on publicly available information, not a collection mission, but the command, not the facilitator, decides whether it needs anything more. Record the decision.

Choosing and adjusting the schedule

The student site lists four schedule options. Choose by the time you can protect, not the time on paper.

D · Four weeks, blendedDispersed units or several time zonesCells drift without contactA shared channel per cell; facilitator checks hand-offs weekly

If you have to cut time

Cut in this order:

  1. Discussion questions: run the two marked "core" in section 5 and skip the rest.
  2. The human-only versus AI-augmented comparison in Phase 2: do it as a whole-room demonstration instead of in every cell.
  3. Capstone track ambition: move cells to Track 1.
  4. Brief length: three minutes per cell instead of five.

Never cut

  • The guardrails brief at the start.
  • Verification of AI output in Phase 2. It is the point of the course.
  • The AI-use-and-verification line in the final product.
  • The AAR.

If you have extra time

  • Lengthen the collection window. A week or more makes Module 01's overload lesson land much harder.
  • Add a red hat/team exercise in Module 02: one cell argues the threat's view of the cable problem (see FM 2-0, paragraph 3-21, and ATP 2-33.4).
  • Hold a 30-day follow-up to see whether any tool is still in use, and why or why not.
04

Running the room

  • Open every session with guardrails. Thirty seconds is enough after the first time: "Unclassified, open sources only, nothing sensitive in an AI tool or a public repo. If in doubt, stop and ask."
  • Grade against the rubric out loud. When a student makes a claim, ask which characteristic it meets or fails. By Module 03 students should do this for each other.
  • Make AI mistakes visible. When a cell catches a hallucination or a tagging error, stop the room and show it. Those moments teach more than any slide.
  • Demonstrate AI tools on the projector, using only the tools the command approved. Show a prompt, show the output, then verify one claim live.
  • Push on likelihood versus confidence. Students blend them. Use ICD 203's language and make them say both, separately.
  • Watch for overattribution. The NORTH WATCH incidents invite "it was obviously sabotage." Ask what evidence would distinguish sabotage from an accident, and whether they have it.
  • Keep cells honest about scope in Module 03. A small tool that works and is documented beats an ambitious one that does not run.
05

Module-by-module notes

For each module: the point, the doctrine link, what good answers to the discussion questions contain, and where cells usually go wrong. "Core" marks the questions to keep when time is short.

Module 01 · The Analyst & the Flood

The point. Judgment comes before automation. Students frame the problem and collect by hand until they feel the overload that Modules 02 and 03 address.

Doctrine link. Plan and direct, and collect (FM 2-0, paragraphs 1-27 to 1-32). Intelligence requirements are primarily PIRs, which the staff breaks into SIRs and indicators. Note FM 2-0's point that the commander holds the authority to task collection assets; the G-2/S-2 informs the collection plan but does not task.

Discussion look-fors

Q1 · core Good answers name a specific expectation, such as "Russia did it" or "it's always an anchor," and a concrete guard: ACH, a devil's advocate, or writing down what evidence would change their mind before collecting.
Q2 Look for design causes: no collection priorities, everyone watching everything, no one owning triage, products measured by volume.
Q3 Any of the three is defensible. Push for a NORTH WATCH-specific failure, such as AIS spoofing feeding an AI summary that states a false position as fact.
Q4 Timeliness breaks first, then relevance: the product answers yesterday's question.
Q5 · core Most cells say predictive is hardest with open sources: intent is rarely visible. Good answers also flag reliable, because of AIS spoofing and crowd maps.
Q6 Framing and collecting usually blur as soon as the first feed is opened. That is normal (FM 2-0, paragraph 1-26) as long as the requirements are updated and written down.
Q7 Look for the link to FM 2-0's "relevant": collect what answers the requirement. A good justification ties every collection effort to a SIR.

Common pitfalls

  • PIRs that are topics, not questions ("Baltic cables"). Make them decision-relevant and time-bound.
  • Indicators nobody can observe in open sources, not marked as gaps.
  • Rating the platform rather than the original source. MarineTraffic is a display; the AIS transponder is the source.
  • Baselines that state assumptions as facts.

Module 02 · Augmenting the Analyst

The point. AI makes the analyst faster only if the analyst verifies it. Students structure the data, use AI for three functions, catch its errors, run ACH, and state likelihood and confidence separately.

Doctrine link. Process and exploit, and analyze and produce (FM 2-0, paragraphs 1-33 to 1-39), the data-literacy note in paragraph 1-34, and the "PED versus analysis and exploitation" vignette in chapter 3. ICD 203 sets the analytic standards for sourcing and uncertainty.

Discussion look-fors

Q1 · core Both readings are defensible. Professionals should calibrate confidence to evidence, not to the tool; flat confidence despite better results can be healthy skepticism or a failure to recognize better work.
Q2 Summarization is usually the most dangerous: it silently drops caveats and merges sources. Entity extraction is a close second (wrong vessel name or IMO number).
Q3 The bright line is classified or CUI information, and anything they would not post publicly with their name on it.
Q4 Look for testable criteria: known error rate on our data, traceable sourcing, a human checkpoint, and someone accountable for it.
Q5 ACH protects against confirmation bias by forcing attention to disconfirming evidence. AI can reintroduce it by agreeing with the analyst's framing or by leading with the most common explanation.
Q6 · core Good answers locate the line: clustering and entity extraction are PED; deciding what the cluster means is analysis. Watch for cells that let the AI summary become the assessment.
Q7 Usually sourcing (not describing source quality) or uncertainty (likelihood and confidence blended).
Q8 FM 2-0's usable improves most from structuring alone, followed by complete.

Common pitfalls

  • Verifying only the claims that look wrong. Require every claim to be marked confirmed, unsupported, or hallucinated.
  • ACH matrices that count consistent evidence. The method works on inconsistency.
  • "Highly likely, with high confidence" as one phrase. Separate them.
  • Pasting whole articles from restricted sources into AI tools. Check the source's terms.

Module 03 · Building the Machine

The point. Automate the repeatable part, keep a human on the judgment, and document both so another analyst can trust or reject the tool.

Doctrine link. Disseminate and integrate, and evaluate and provide feedback. FM 2-0 says the Army "will likely implement fully in the near future" automated processing, and requires data that is visible, accessible, understandable, linked, trustworthy, interoperable, and secure (paragraph 1-34).

Discussion look-fors

Q1 · core The human stays on interpretation, on any judgment about intent, and on what goes to the commander.
Q2 Reasonable unattended actions: pulling and filtering a feed, flagging a rule-based anomaly. Never automated: attributing intent, or sending a product to the commander.
Q3 · core A good answer names the failure mode (a missed vessel because of an AIS gap), who notices (the analyst checking the flag list against GFW gap events), and how fast (the next watch).
Q4 Common single changes: a measured error rate, source traceability on every output, or a named owner.
Q5 The tool usually improves timely, usable, and complete, and endangers accurate and reliable. Good designs show sources and confidence next to every flag.
Q6 Most student tools fail "linked" and "interoperable": outputs nobody else's system can ingest. A consistent schema and CSV or JSON export fixes most of it.

Build clinic tips

  • Check every repo for keys and raw licensed data before it goes public. Look in notebook outputs too.
  • Track 1 cells finish fastest. Have them help other cells test their tools.
  • If a Track 3 app will not deploy, accept a working notebook plus a screenshot and grade the analysis.

Answer keys and worked examples

Example requirements (Phase 1)

One defensible framing. Accept others that are decision-relevant and observable.

PIR: Will a vessel deliberately damage undersea cables or pipelines in the Gulf of Finland or central Baltic in the next 30 days?

Candidate incidents for the ACH (Phase 2)

Pick one and build a one-page fact sheet from current reporting before the event. These summaries are starting points only. Investigations and legal proceedings on several of them continued after the initial reporting, so check the current status and cite your sources. The disagreement among official findings is the lesson.

ACH scaffold

What a strong cell's matrix usually contains, whichever incident you choose:

  • Diagnostic evidence: how long the anchor dragged (kilometres of seabed scarring point away from a brief accident); speed and course changes at the moment of damage; whether the crew reported the anchor problem; weather and sea state; the vessel's maintenance record.
  • Non-diagnostic evidence (consistent with every hypothesis): the vessel's flag, its ownership network, and the political climate. These matter for the PIR but do not distinguish sabotage from an accident.
  • Evidence to treat with care: official attributions made quickly and later revised; social media claims; AIS positions during known GNSS interference.
  • A good conclusion ranks every hypothesis, states likelihood and confidence separately, names the two or three items it depends on, and lists what would change it.

Score each cell out of 25. The OPSEC and data-rights check is a gate: a cell that fails it does not pass until the repo is fixed, whatever its score. Grade analytic value, not code volume.

08

Safety stops

Stop the activity and act immediately if any of these happens:

  • Classified or CUI information appears on an unclassified device, in an AI tool, or in a repo. Stop work on that device and notify the security manager under your unit's spillage procedures. Do not try to clean it up yourself.
  • A student collects or keeps information about a U.S. person outside what the authorities permit. Stop, do not disseminate it, and consult the oversight officer (DODM 5240.01, procedures 2 to 4; AR 381-10).
  • A student engages with a target: messaging, following, joining groups, or creating cover accounts. NORTH WATCH is passive viewing of public information only.
  • A repo goes public with keys or raw licensed data. Make it private, rotate the key, and remove the data from the history.
  • Anyone is unsure whether a task is permitted. Pause it and ask. That is the right answer, not a failure.

Troubleshooting

ProblemFix
A tracker or AI tool is blocked on the networkUse the substitute the dry run identified (for example, VesselFinder for MarineTraffic, adsb.lol for ADS-B Exchange), or switch to the devices the command approved.
Free API rate limit hit (OpenSky, GDELT)Cache results to a CSV in the repo for the session and query less often. Rate limits are a real-world constraint worth discussing.
A live feed shows nothing interestingThat is a valid result. Discuss the absence of evidence, then widen the area or window, or switch to the incident fact sheet.
GitHub Pages site does not appearRepo Settings › Pages › deploy from branch main, folder / (root); make sure the file is named index.html; wait a few minutes.
Colab session reset and work lostSave the notebook to the GitHub repo after every block. Make this a rule from the start.
Streamlit app is asleep or will not deployWake it before the brief. If it will not deploy, grade the notebook and a screenshot.
A student cannot create a GitHub account on the networkPair them with a cell member's repo as a collaborator and fix the account afterwards.
10

AAR and follow-up

This is the course's own "evaluate and provide feedback" step. Run it before anyone leaves.

AAR questions (20 to 30 minutes)

  1. What was supposed to happen? What actually happened? Why was there a difference?
  2. Which characteristic of effective intelligence did AI help most? Which did it put most at risk?
  3. What AI error did your cell catch, and how? What might you have missed?
  4. Which tool or tactic will you actually use at work next month?
  5. What should we sustain and improve in this OPD next time?

After the event

  • Collect repo links and record capstone scores.
  • File AAR notes with the S-2 and update this guide and the site (open an issue or pull request on the course repo).
  • Check that every public student repo passed the OPSEC and data-rights gate.
  • Optional 30-day follow-up: is any tool still running? What stopped the others?
